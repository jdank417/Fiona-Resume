Educational Philosophy

what I believe about teaching & learning

“I hope to be the teacher who makes every child feel important and believes in them when they struggle to believe in themselves.”

I believe education is about much more than teaching academic content. It is about helping children grow while developing the confidence and skills they will carry throughout their lives. Every child deserves to enter a classroom feeling valued, respected, included, and safe. I want my students to know their voices matter and that they are capable of achieving great things, even when they may not yet believe that themselves. When children feel supported, they are more willing to take chances, ask questions, and learn from mistakes.

My philosophy is influenced by progressivism, particularly John Dewey's belief that children learn through meaningful experiences and active problem solving. Ornstein and Levine (2008) explain that Dewey viewed learning as a process in which students encounter problems, gather information, consider solutions, and test their ideas. This connects to my belief that learning should be engaging and connected to everyday life. Rather than simply memorizing information, students should explore ideas and apply what they learn. Williams (2017) also explains that Dewey viewed the classroom as a social environment where students learn and solve problems together. Additionally, I connect with Maria Montessori's approach, which emphasizes independence and hands-on learning with teachers guiding students as they explore (Williams, 2017).

Building genuine relationships with students is one of my most important responsibilities as an educator. I want to know each child as an individual, including their interests, strengths, challenges, and background. Since no two students are alike, supporting them will not always look the same. To me, treating students fairly does not mean treating them exactly alike; it means making sure each child has what they need to succeed. I will adapt instruction and provide additional support or challenges when necessary. I also want students to feel that their cultures, backgrounds, and experiences are valued. Williams (2017) emphasizes that education should support the growth of the whole child, not just academic achievement. I never want a student to feel embarrassed for asking for help or learning differently.

A positive and structured classroom environment is also essential. I want my classroom to feel welcoming while maintaining consistent routines and clear expectations. When a student struggles with behavior, I would speak with them privately rather than embarrass them in front of classmates. I also hope to build a classroom community through morning meetings and traditions that give students opportunities to connect. Williams (2017) explains that morning meetings can help establish trust and a sense of belonging. Through hands-on activities, classroom discussions, and small group instruction, I hope to make learning engaging while recognizing that students learn in different ways. I will use observations and students' work to monitor their progress, provide feedback, and adjust my instruction when needed.

Ultimately, I want students to leave my classroom with more than academic knowledge. Success should also be measured by personal growth. A student who comes out of their shell, gains confidence, or finds the courage to participate has accomplished something worth celebrating. I want students to recognize their progress and appreciate perspectives different from their own. Through collaboration and meaningful learning experiences, they can see how supporting others makes a difference. My hope is that we work together, have fun, and leave our classroom in June as better people than when we entered in September.

Teaching requires patience and a willingness to keep learning. I plan to seek feedback, reflect on my practices, and learn from colleagues and students. Above all, I hope to be the teacher who makes every child feel important and believes in them when they struggle to believe in themselves. If my students leave knowing they are valued, capable, supported, and that they belong, I will know I have made a meaningful difference.

References: Ornstein, A. C., & Levine, D. U. (2008). Foundations of education (10th ed.). Wadsworth Cengage Learning. · Williams, M. K. (2017). John Dewey in the 21st century. Journal of Inquiry and Action in Education, 9(1), 91–102.
